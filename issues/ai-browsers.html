Deep Dive Nº 02 · Agentic Browsers

The browser that clicks for you

Chrome, Comet and Claude can now navigate websites, fill in forms and buy things on your behalf. That is a real shortcut through uni admin, and a brand-new way to get robbed. Here is the balance.

The Student AI Brief 6 min read 19 July 2026 Deep Dive series

For thirty years a web browser did exactly one thing: it showed you a page and waited for you to act. You read, you clicked, you typed. The browser never touched the controls. That assumption quietly broke this year. The browser now reads the page for you, decides what to click, and clicks it. This new species has a name, the agentic browser, and by mid-2026 the three biggest names in software have all shipped one.

An agentic browser is an ordinary browser with an AI agent wired into the driving seat. You give it a goal in plain English, "find me the cheapest train to Manchester next Friday and hold a seat", and it opens tabs, reads results, fills boxes and presses buttons until the job is done. The interesting questions are no longer whether this works. It works. The questions are what it is genuinely good for, and what happens when a web page it visits turns out to be lying to it.

What just arrivedThe browsers that now act for you

The clearest signal that this went mainstream: on 28 January 2026, Google added an agentic mode called auto browse to Chrome, powered by its Gemini 3 model. It rolled out as a preview to Google AI Pro and AI Ultra subscribers in the US, and it will happily book hotels, fill out forms from a PDF, collect tax documents, chase quotes from tradespeople and renew licences. Crucially, Google built in a brake: auto browse is designed to pause and ask for confirmation before it does anything hard to undo, such as making a purchase or posting on your behalf.

Chrome was not first. Perplexity's Comet, a Chromium browser with an agent living in the sidebar, reached desktop in 2025 and completed its run to iOS in March 2026. It reads the tab you are on, holds context across a session, and can compare products or complete a booking without you. Anthropic's Claude for Chrome, an extension that turns Claude into both a page-reading sidebar and an agent that clicks and types across tabs, moved into beta for paid subscribers over the same window. Three approaches, one idea: the model has hands now.

Under the bonnetThe plumbing that made it viable

None of this appeared from nowhere. For years the unglamorous groundwork was being laid by tools most students have never heard of. Underneath almost every browser agent sits Playwright (and its older sibling Puppeteer), the open-source libraries that let code drive a real Chrome instance: click this element, type into that field, wait for the page to load.

On top of that sit the newer companies that rent out browsers by the thousand. Browserbase runs fleets of cloud Chrome sessions on demand, used by the likes of Perplexity and Vercel, and ships an open framework called Stagehand for AI-driven automation. Steel offers an open-source browser API built specifically for agents, and the popular browser-use project glues a language model to Playwright in a few lines of Python. The pattern that turned a research demo into a shipping product was boring but decisive: give the model a reliable, disposable browser it can poke at, and a safe way to see what happened.

The browser stopped being a window you look through. It became a hand you point at the web.

The student caseWhere it genuinely helps

Strip away the hype and there is a real, dull, valuable core here, and university life is full of exactly the tasks these agents handle best: repetitive, form-heavy, spread across too many tabs. Sensible uses:

  • Research triage. Ask it to open ten sources on a topic, pull the key claims and hand you a cited summary with the links, so you decide what to actually read rather than opening thirty tabs yourself.
  • Form-filling. Student finance renewals, accommodation applications, society sign-ups and society AGM RSVPs are mostly the same details typed into different boxes. An agent reading from one document and filling the rest is a real time saver.
  • Comparison. Cheapest railcard, best-value laptop within a budget, which library has the textbook in stock: tasks that mean visiting several sites and holding numbers in your head.
  • Admin chasing. Checking a portal for exam timetables, deadline changes or whether a payment landed, the low-stakes lookups that eat an afternoon.
The rule of thumb

Let an agent do anything that is tedious and reversible, and keep it away from anything irreversible or private. Booking a library room is reversible. Paying a deposit, sending an email from your account, or logging into your bank is not. That line is the whole game.

The trapdoorHow a web page can hijack your agent

Here is the part the marketing skips. A traditional browser never trusted a web page to give it orders, only content to display. An agentic browser reads the page as instructions to interpret, and that is a genuinely new attack surface. It is called indirect prompt injection: a malicious page hides commands where you will never see them, in white text on a white background, inside HTML comments, in an image caption or a tab title, and your agent reads them as if they came from you.

The attacks are not theoretical. Palo Alto's Unit 42 has documented injections in the wild that made agents like Comet perform sensitive cross-site actions when simply asked to summarise a page. Google reported a 32% rise in malicious pages of this kind between November 2025 and February 2026, measured across billions of crawled pages a month. And in a case that made the pattern concrete, researchers found a flaw nicknamed ShadowPrompt in Anthropic's Claude Chrome extension: chained together, an over-permissive origin rule and a cross-site scripting bug let any website silently inject prompts into a user's Claude session. Anthropic patched it in extension version 1.0.41 after disclosure in late December 2025, but the lesson stands.

The vendors are measuring the problem honestly, which is the encouraging part. Anthropic's own red-team numbers show how much work this takes:

0%

Read those last two together. Defences have gone from letting one attack in three through to stopping almost all of them, and that is real progress. But almost is the operative word. As Anthropic puts it plainly, no browser agent is immune to prompt injection, and a one-in-a-hundred failure rate is still a live risk when the agent has access to your inbox or your card.

Staying safeRules for letting an agent loose

You do not need to avoid these tools. You need to use them the way you would lend your laptop to a clever but gullible friend. A short checklist:

  • Never sign it into your bank or primary email. The worst outcomes almost all involve an agent with access to money or messages being talked into using it. If it cannot reach your bank, a hijacked page cannot drain it.
  • Keep confirmation gates on. Chrome's auto browse pauses before purchases and posts by design. Leave that on. If a tool lets an agent buy or send without asking, that is a reason to distrust it, not a convenience.
  • Prefer read-only tasks. Summarising, comparing and researching are far safer than acting. The more an agent can only look and report, the smaller the blast radius when something goes wrong.
  • Watch what it does. These agents narrate their steps. Skim the actions before you approve them, especially anything involving a login, a payment field or an unfamiliar site.

The takeawayHow to think about it

The agentic browser is the first genuinely new thing to happen to the browser in a decade, and the market clearly believes it: Chrome, Perplexity and Anthropic do not all ship the same feature in the same six months by accident. For a student drowning in portals and forms, that is real leverage, the boring admin of university life handed to something that does not mind doing it.

But the same property that makes it useful, that it treats the open web as instructions to follow, is exactly what makes it dangerous. A calculator cannot be tricked by the numbers you feed it. An agent can be tricked by the page it reads. Hold both ideas at once: let it do the tedious, reversible work, keep it away from your money and your identity, and never forget that every page it visits is a page that might be talking to it instead of to you.
